# cold-outreach-craft

A stranger owes you nothing. They will give you one line of attention. Everything here is about earning the second line, then the reply. Cold outreach is not marketing — it's a personal message from one competent person to another, and it has to read that way.

Every high-performing cold message has exactly three parts: a research-backed opener, one clear value proposition, and a low-friction ask (SmartLead, Autobound, 2026). Nothing else. Always run drafts through **write-human** (a cold email full of AI tells is dead on arrival) and borrow structure from **business-email**.

---

## Rule 1 — Earn the first line

The first line is the whole game. If it could have been sent to 5,000 people, it will be read by none. It must prove, instantly, that you looked at *them*.

- **Never open about yourself.** "I'm the founder of X" / "My company helps businesses to..." is a delete. They don't care yet.
- **Never open with flattery-by-template.** "I've been following your amazing work" is a tell that you followed nothing.
- **Open with one true, specific observation about them** (Rule 2). That's the pattern-interrupt: it looks like a real person wrote it just for them, because someone did.

---

## Rule 2 — The one real specific observation

Personalized openers get up to ~142% higher reply rates than generic ones (Martal, 2025). But "personalized" doesn't mean merge-fielding their first name. It means one concrete thing that required you to actually look.

Good observations are specific enough that they'd make the reader think *"this person actually looked into my situation"*:

- A thing they shipped/posted/said recently, named exactly. "Saw you moved the clinic to the new Newport location last month —"
- A specific detail of their operation. "You're running three IV bars but only listing peptides on one site —"
- A visible consequence you can infer. "Your team page went from 4 to 11 since spring — that kind of growth usually breaks intake scheduling first."

Bad (fake) observations: "I love your website," "Your company is doing great things," "I noticed you're in [industry]." These are worse than nothing because they signal a template wearing a costume.

**One** observation. Not three. Then move.

---

## Rule 3 — Curiosity over pitch

You are not trying to close in the email. You are trying to make them curious enough to reply. The pitch happens in the conversation the email starts.

- State the value as an outcome they'd want, not a feature list. Not "we offer 99% pure RUO peptides with COAs" → "most clinics we talk to are overpaying their current supplier by 30-40% on the same compounds, same purity docs."
- Leave a gap. "There's a reason your reorder costs are creeping up and it's not the peptide" makes them want the next line. A full explanation removes the reason to reply.
- Don't dump credentials, links, and attachments in a first touch. One credential, if any (Rule 6). Links can trip spam filters and read as blast.

---

## Rule 4 — Brevity discipline

- **Under 125 words. 50-125 is the reply sweet spot** (Growth List, 2026). Shorter is usually better for first touch.
- It should read in under 10 seconds and be answerable in one.
- No preamble, no signature paragraph, no "I hope this finds you well." Observation → value → ask. Done.
- On a phone it's one screen, no scroll. That's the real length limit.
- Every sentence earns its place or dies. A cold email has no room for a sentence that's only there for politeness.

---

## Rule 5 — The soft single CTA

One ask. Not two, not three (SmartLead). Low-commitment asks get about 2x the replies of an aggressive "book a 30-minute call" (Autobound).

- Ask for interest or a tiny step, not their calendar: "Worth a quick look?" / "Want me to send the price list for the three you use most?" / "Should I put together a quick comparison for your top SKUs?"
- Make "no" cheap and "yes" one word. A question they can answer with "sure" or "not now" outperforms a demand.
- Never stack: "Let me know if you're interested, and also feel free to book a call, and I can send over a deck too." That's three asks; it gets zero.
- The CTA is a question with a question mark. If your close is a statement ("Looking forward to connecting"), it isn't a CTA.

---

## Rule 6 — Honest credibility: lead with value or a real credential, never hype

Trust is the scarce resource. You build it by being specific and real, not by adjectives.

- **Lead with genuine value:** give them something useful in the email itself — a relevant number, a spotted problem, a quick teardown of one thing. "Value-first" outreach earns the reply because you've already been useful before asking for anything.
- **Or lead with a real, checkable credential:** "we supply 40+ clinics in TX and can send COAs on any lot" beats "we're the industry-leading trusted provider." A number, a name, a proof — never a superlative.
- **Never fake scarcity or authority.** "Only 3 spots left," "As seen in," borrowed logos, invented urgency — a sharp reader smells it and you're done.
- If you don't have a credential yet, lead with value harder. Being visibly useful is its own credential.

---

## Rule 7 — Follow-up cadence

Most replies come from follow-ups, not the first send — 40-60% of positive replies come from touches #2 and #3 (SalesCaptain, 2025). But each one must carry a new angle. "Just following up" is noise and trains them to ignore you.

A workable 3-4 touch sequence:

- **Touch 1 (day 0):** the observation + value + soft ask.
- **Touch 2 (day 3-4):** a *new* hook — a different angle on the value, a relevant proof point, or a smaller ask. Not a repeat. "Different thought — even if the supplier switch isn't now, here's the one COA question worth asking whoever you use today: [x]."
- **Touch 3 (day 7-10):** the easy-out / break-up. "I'll stop here — is this a not-now, or a not-for-us? Either's fine, just don't want to clutter your inbox." Break-up emails get surprisingly high reply rates because they're honest and give control back.
- **Stop at 3-4.** Persistence past that is spam, not craft.

Keep each follow-up on the same thread. Keep each one shorter than the last.

## Full examples

**Cold email — B2B, value-led**
> Subject: your reorder costs (Loop / RUO)
>
> Rachel — saw the second Dallas location go live on your site last week. Nice.
>
> Most clinics scaling to a second site get quietly overcharged on peptides because the first supplier knows switching is a hassle. We run the same compounds, third-party COA on every lot, usually 30-40% under the incumbent.
>
> Want me to send a quick price comparison on the three you list? Takes me five minutes, no call needed.
>
> — [you]

Why: real specific observation, one value gap left open, one credential (COA on every lot), one soft ask, under 90 words, no hype.

**LinkedIn connection note (300-char limit)**
> Rachel — noticed you just opened the second Dallas clinic. Congrats. I work with RUO peptide supply for longevity clinics and usually save the second-location folks a real chunk on reorders. Not pitching here — happy to send a price comparison if it's useful.

Why: fits the limit, specific, disarms ("not pitching here"), offers value, no link.

**The break-up (touch 3)**
> Rachel — I'll leave it here. Is this a not-right-now, or a not-for-us? Either's a fine answer — just don't want to keep landing in your inbox.

Why: honest, gives control, one-word-answerable, ends the sequence cleanly.

## Before / after

**1 — the me-first opener**

Before:
> Hi Rachel, my name is [x] and I'm the founder of [company], a leading provider of high-quality research compounds. We help clinics like yours streamline their supply chain and unlock significant savings. I'd love to hop on a 30-minute call to walk you through our solutions!

After:
> Rachel — saw the second Dallas location go live last week. Clinics at that stage usually get quietly overcharged on peptide reorders. We run the same compounds with COAs on every lot, typically 30-40% under the incumbent. Worth a quick price comparison?

Why: killed the self-intro, "leading provider," "streamline," "unlock," and the 30-minute demand. Led with a real observation, one credential, one soft ask.

**2 — the fake-personal**

Before:
> Hi there! I came across your profile and was really impressed by all the amazing work you're doing in the healthcare space. I think there could be some great synergy between our companies!

After:
> Rachel — you're listing peptides on the Newport site but not the two Dallas ones yet. If that's a supply gap and not a choice, that's usually the exact thing we fix. Want the details?

Why: "impressed by your amazing work" and "synergy" are template tells. The rewrite names a specific, checkable detail that could only come from looking.

**3 — the stacked CTA**

Before:
> Let me know if you'd be interested in learning more! You can book a call using the link below, or feel free to reply here, and I've also attached our full product catalog and pricing deck for your reference. Looking forward to connecting!

After:
> Want me to send the price comparison on the three SKUs you already list? Just say the word.

Why: four asks and two attachments collapsed to one tiny, one-word-answerable ask. No links to trip filters, no deck to ignore.

## Ship checklist

- [ ] First line is about them, not you.
- [ ] Exactly one real, specific, checkable observation — no fake flattery.
- [ ] Value stated as an outcome, with a curiosity gap, not a feature dump.
- [ ] Under 125 words; one phone screen; readable in 10 seconds.
- [ ] One soft, low-friction, one-word-answerable CTA with a question mark.
- [ ] One real credential or genuine value — zero hype, zero fake scarcity.
- [ ] No links/attachments in first touch unless essential.
- [ ] Follow-ups each carry a new angle; sequence caps at 3-4; ends with an honest break-up.
- [ ] Run through write-human (no tells) and cross-check business-email structure.
